# The campaign is built like a modern serial: a mythology across seasons, arcs with anchor episodes, adventures with lines A, B and C, and encounters that turn

## Summary

This record is the story design for all three levels. The owner set the shape on 2026-11-01 and supplied notes on screenwriting and serial television. The campaign is a series with a logline, a mythology and a plan of what is revealed when. An arc is a season with anchor adventures: premiere, midpoint, penultimate and finale. An adventure is an episode with a logline and three lines: A closes in the episode, B carries relationships, and C moves the mythology. It follows Snyder's 15 beats mapped to its encounters and ends on a fair cliffhanger. An encounter is a scene: a tactical goal, a conflict of intentions and a turn of polarity. A setup ledger, a ticking clock and antagonists who believe they're right run through all levels. Code checks whatever a field can prove, and the critic judges the rest. For a child, "all is lost" means a plan collapsing, never a death, and a cliffhanger stops at what the creepiness level allows. This is craft practice and no study shows it serves a child; only playing will tell.

## The question

What must each level hold, so that generators, the critic and the Master write by the best practice of screen and serial writing, and how does a game with a child change it?

Two assumptions need a challenge. Film structure assumes one viewer watching passively, and here a child's actions move the story, so a beat is a target and the Master may reach it another way. Film also leans on death and despair, which the world forbids.

## Method

Read on 2026-11-01: the owner's notes pasted into the session, on Snyder's beats, Field's three acts, the hero's journey, character arcs, serial lines, season anchors, cliffhangers, clocks, setups, scene polarity, antagonists, subtext, MacGuffins, showing, exposition, supporting characters and loglines. Those notes are the owner's summaries of those authors, so I cite them as such. RES-4316 holds what I read directly. Nothing was tested with a player.

## Findings

Claims here come from the owner's notes unless marked [RES-4316] or [Inference].

### Beats give a precise frame

Snyder's 15 beats run from an opening image through theme, set-up, catalyst and debate, then break into two, B story, fun and games, midpoint, bad guys close in, all is lost and dark night of the soul, then break into three, finale and a final image that mirrors the first. Field's model divides 25, 50 and 25 percent. [Inference] An adventure of 10 to 20 encounters can't give each beat an encounter, so several beats share one.

### Serials run lines at different speeds

Line A is the episode's conflict and closes in it. Line B is relationships and flows across episodes. Line C is the mythology of the season, a couple of scenes that move the global story. Later series bind episodes tighter than the older one-problem model.

### A season has anchors

A premiere sets the status quo and the global conflict. A midpoint breaks the story and raises the stakes. The penultimate episode holds the dark night and the largest climax. The finale settles the consequences, fixes a new world and opens a question for the next season.

### A cliffhanger poses a new question

It radically changes the context, and it must be prepared by what came before. Micro versions break expectation, spring an ambush or reveal a clue. Macro versions close A and open C, or change the meaning of what came before. A fair exit rests on resources, skills or facts stated earlier, and it costs effort or sacrifice. A fake one that resolves as no danger destroys trust.

### Clocks, setups and scenes

A ticking clock turns a threat into a limit, and it works best when events shorten it. A setup must work in its own moment and hide as a joke or a habit, and its payoff must feel inevitable. Each scene is a small story, with a tactical goal, opposed intentions and a turn of polarity, and one without a turn is mere information. Before an ending, A reaches a deadlock, and what the hero gains in B gives the key to A.

### People

A deep antagonist believes they're right, has a coherent logic and is competent. Supporting characters mirror the hero's question: allies show what she can become, and broken ones show the cost. A hero wins early sympathy by a small act of kindness or self-irony. Arcs are positive, flat or negative, by whether the hero overcomes a false belief. The hero's journey adds a mentor, a threshold and an ordeal.

### Telling

Subtext hides what a character wants under what they say. A MacGuffin only moves the plot, and the real goal is inner. Showing beats telling. Exposition comes in small doses, through action, and rules appear when the hero hits them. A logline names no names: when an event happens, a described hero must reach a measurable goal against an obstacle, or a stated loss follows.

### Twists, endings, voice and stages

A twist rests on hidden setups, can use a red herring, rewrites what earlier scenes meant, and hits the hero's false belief, which pushes the third act. A closed ending answers the dramatic question, and an open one leaves the outer question while the inner change is done, unlike a cliffhanger. A voiceover earns its place by contrast: an unreliable narrator, a voice from later, or hidden thoughts. A synopsis of a page or two tests the story's direction, and a treatment finds structural holes before dialogue.

### A character bible

A bible fixes each person's want against their need, a ghost from the past that shaped their defence, a lie about how the world works, a speech print that identifies a line with no name beside it, skills and blind spots, how they reflect others, and an arc from a starting state to an end state.

### What changes for this game

[Inference] The heroine's arc is positive, and her false belief concerns the story's world and never the player. A dark night stays short: a calm, a helper and a way on. A clock counts world time in the scene, never real time. A goal is concrete enough for a child to say aloud. Subtext stays readable. A mythology fits CAN-0080's question of who knitted the Tower. Her actions can move a beat, so the Master holds it as a target.

### The character card

The library's character card holds `want`, `need`, `wound`, `falseBelief`, `voice`, `skills`, `weakSpots`, `relations` and `arc`. A wound suits a children's story: a lost friend who moved away or an old failure, and no trauma. The heroine has a card without a fixed `voice`, because the player speaks for her. The Master reads the card when the person is in the scene, so voices hold across generations.

### Twists and stages

An arc plans its twist in `reveals`, with the setups that prepare it and a false trail where it fits. The loop writes logline, synopsis, treatment and beat sheet in turn, and the critic reads the treatment before encounters are written, because a hole found there costs far less than one found in a finished graph. The Master is the storyteller's voice, and it may add a layer the scene can't show, such as what the heroine will remember later.

### The Master's rules

The system prompt tells the Master to run each encounter as a scene with a turn, to show and not tell, to give rules when she hits them, to keep subtext readable, to let new people earn sympathy, to tell the world's rules in action, and to end with the prepared cliffhanger. It may reach a beat another way when she acts differently.

### Limits for a child

All is lost breaks a plan, a friendship for a moment or a hope. Nobody dies. The dark night is brief, with a helper near. A cliffhanger ends on a question and a promise, within the creepiness level. It never leaves her afraid at the end of a day, so a fright-based cliffhanger waits for the next morning's scene only at level 0 or above by the parent's choice.

## Conclusions

1. Campaign, arc and adventure each hold a logline by the formula, with no names.
2. The campaign holds a theme, the heroine's arc, a mythology and a plan of reveals.
3. An arc holds anchors for premiere, midpoint, penultimate and finale, and a closing question.
4. Each adventure has a kind: mythology, case, character or filler.
5. An adventure holds lines A, B and C, and A closes in it.
6. An adventure's encounters follow the 15 beats, several of them sharing an encounter.
7. An adventure holds a clock that events can shorten, except a filler.
8. A setup ledger crosses all levels, and a setup is planted before it pays.
9. Each adventure ends on a cliffhanger prepared by what came before, with an honest way out.
10. An opposing force has a logic of its own and believes it is right.
11. An encounter is a scene with a tactical goal, opposed intentions, a turn of polarity and readable subtext.
12. The Master shows and doesn't tell, gives rules when she hits them, and may reach a beat another way.
13. Code checks what a field proves, and the critic judges the rest.
14. All is lost never means a death, a dark night stays short, and a cliffhanger stays within the creepiness level and leaves no fright at the end of a day.
15. The studio shows a mythology map of questions, clues and answers.
16. A twist is prepared by setups the player could have seen, and a major one strikes the heroine's false belief.
17. An arc's ending states whether it closes its question or leaves it open.
18. A character's library entry holds a want, a need, a wound, a false belief, a voice, skills, weak spots, relations and an arc.
19. The pipeline writes logline, synopsis, treatment and beat sheet before encounters, and the critic reads the treatment first.
